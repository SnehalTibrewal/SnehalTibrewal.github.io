Research

A gravitational-wave signal is a compressed record of two compact objects and their orbit. I’m interested in reading that record faithfully, and in noticing when we might be fooling ourselves: what orbital eccentricity adds to a signal, where Bayesian inference is reliable, and how far the waveform models we use drift from numerical relativity.

Misinterpreting spin precession as orbital eccentricity in gravitational-wave signals

S. Tibrewal, A. Zimmerman, J. Lange, D. Shoemaker
Phys. Rev. D 114, 064046 (2026)

Heavy black-hole binaries give us only a few cycles of signal, so a wobbling orbital plane (spin precession) might be mistaken for an eccentric orbit. We tested this by analysing precessing signals with eccentric models. The mix-up can happen, but only in a narrow slice of parameter space, and it vanishes once the viewing angle changes.

Impact of eccentricity and higher modes on neutron star–black hole parameter estimation

S. Tibrewal, A. Jan, A. Zimmerman, H.-Y. Chen
arXiv:2607.16135 (2026)

In neutron star–black hole mergers, the masses and spins blur together in the data. We asked whether a slightly eccentric orbit helps tell them apart, using signals modelled on GW200105. It does: eccentricity sharpens the mass and spin measurements by more than tenfold, while higher-order modes add little at this loudness.
